LakeBand

LakeBand forecasts where lake-effect snow bands off Lakes Erie and Ontario will move over the next three hours. Each time a radar finishes a scan, it outlines every band it can see, then forecasts each band’s position from 15 minutes to 3 hours ahead. A neural network trained on ten winters of storms makes the forecast from the band’s recent motion on radar, HRRR winds and lake-effect ingredients, lake surface temperatures, and the radar’s own wind profile.

How well it does

LakeBand was graded once, on a winter it never saw during development (2019–20: 15 storms, 35,917 forecasts), against persistence: every band stays exactly where it is now. Persistence is hard to beat in the first hours, because lake-effect bands often hold their position.

When the wind shifts and bands swing, which is when persistence fails, the gain is larger: a 30% smaller error at 2 hours (13.8 km against 19.8 km, 807 bands). LakeBand beat persistence in 12 of 14 groups of storms.

Forecasts update with every radar scan. The 3-hour forecast needs a later HRRR hour, so during the first half of each hour it comes from a scan up to 30 minutes older, shown with its true valid time. The scorecard on the page tracks the live forecasts against where the bands actually went.

Past storms

The Past storms button replays seven notable storms from the training winters, scan by scan, with each forecast drawn beside where the band actually went. Their forecasts come from cross-validation versions of LakeBand, each trained without that storm, so they show how the model does on weather it has not seen; the live model trained on all of them. On these seven storms the forecast position error was 23–34% smaller than persistence at 1 and 2 hours. They were picked for being notable, not as a test sample.

Reading the map

Outlines show the bands on the latest scan and where each is forecast to be at the lead you choose; Play steps through the leads. The dashed line is the training area: LakeBand finds and forecasts bands only inside it, where the lowest radar beam is under about 2 km, and it can be switched off. Inside it you see the radar LakeBand read; outside it, the NOAA MRMS mosaic (each place seen by the lowest beam of the nearest radars), for context. Comparisons with persistence and the scorecard are under Verification.

Important limitations

LakeBand forecasts where existing bands go. It does not forecast how much snow falls or where new bands will form, and radar reflectivity is not a snowfall rate. If a model input arrives late, the forecast falls back to persistence and says so on the page.

It only sees bands where a radar’s lowest beam is under about 2 km, within roughly 120 km of the radar. That leaves a gap over Erie, Pennsylvania, about 145 km from the Buffalo radar and 165 km from Cleveland’s: a band sitting there is outlined only where it reaches the covered shores, though the live map shows its echo from the MRMS mosaic. The far western end of Lake Erie, where bands rarely form, is outside the area LakeBand processes.
