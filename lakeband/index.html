LakeBand

LakeBand forecasts where lake-effect snow bands off Lakes Erie and Ontario will move over the next three hours. Each time a radar finishes a scan, it outlines every band it can see, then forecasts each band’s position from 15 minutes to 3 hours ahead. A neural network trained on ten winters of storms makes the forecast from the band’s recent motion on radar, HRRR winds and lake-effect ingredients, lake surface temperatures, and the radar’s own wind profile.

How well it does

LakeBand was graded once, inside its training area, on a winter it never saw during development (2019–20: 15 storms, 35,917 forecasts), against persistence: every band stays exactly where it is now. Persistence is hard to beat in the first hours, because lake-effect bands often hold their position.

When the wind shifts and bands swing, which is when persistence fails, the gain is larger: a 30% smaller error at 2 hours (13.8 km against 19.8 km, 807 bands). LakeBand beat persistence in 12 of 14 groups of storms.

Forecasts update with every radar scan. The 3-hour forecast needs a later HRRR hour, so during the first half of each hour it comes from a scan up to 30 minutes older, shown with its true valid time. The scorecard on the page tracks the live forecasts against where the bands actually went.

Past storms

The Past storms button replays seven notable storms from the training winters. Each was run through LakeBand’s real-time engine on its own archived radar, scan by scan, as live: bands over the whole of both lakes and a forecast at every scan. Every forecast comes from a cross-validation version of LakeBand trained without that storm, so they show how the model does on weather it has not seen; the live model trained on all of them. For the three storms before 2020 the model’s weather inputs were rebuilt from the public archives, as the live engine builds them. Inside the training area, under Verification, you can draw each forecast beside where the band actually went; there, by LakeBand’s own verification, the forecast position error was 23–34% smaller than persistence at 1 and 2 hours. Forecasts beyond the dashed line are not verified. The storms were picked for being notable, not as a test sample.

Reading the map

Outlines show the bands on the latest scan and where each is forecast to be at the lead you choose; Play steps through the leads. Bands are found and forecast over the whole of both lakes and drawn the same everywhere. The dashed line marks the training area, where the model was trained and graded and the lowest radar beam is under about 2 km; it can be switched off. Inside it you see the radar LakeBand read; beyond it, the NOAA MRMS mosaic (each place seen by the lowest beam of the nearest radars). Comparisons with persistence and the scorecard are under Verification. To follow one band, tap it and choose Isolate: the map then shows only that band, the radar inside its outline and its forecast, scan after scan, until you choose Show all or press Escape.

Important limitations

LakeBand forecasts where existing bands go. It does not forecast how much snow falls or where new bands will form, and radar reflectivity is not a snowfall rate. If a model input arrives late, the forecast falls back to persistence and says so on the page.

Its scores were measured inside the training area only; beyond it the same model runs on the same kind of radar data, but how well it does there has not been measured. It sees bands where a radar’s lowest beam is under about 3 km above the ground, which covers nearly all of both lakes. Over Erie, Pennsylvania, about 145 km from the Buffalo radar and 165 km from Cleveland’s, the beam is 2 to 3 km up and passes over the top of shallow snow, so a band there can look weaker or be missed. Objects seen only by a beam 2 to 3 km up are less certain: in a hand check of 48 of them, about 1 in 3 was a real band.
